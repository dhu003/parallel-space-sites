Methodology

Last updated: November 21, 2026

This page is the working method behind the three free Parallel Space calculators: the Small Business Acquisition Calculator, the What Business Can I Afford? calculator, and the SBA Funding Finder. It states what the numbers are, where they came from, and what they are not. If a number here is wrong, or a source has moved, tell us and we will fix it.

1. What the presets are

Each of the 19 industries is a researched operating model: revenue drivers, operating ratios, a sale price, and working-capital terms. A preset is an average of businesses like the one you are looking at — not any specific business’s financials. The first thing the tools ask you to do, in every preset’s own note, is replace the averages with the business’s actual collected revenue and actual expenses.

The presets are built the same way every time: drivers produce revenue (washes per day, trucks and miles, caregivers and billable hours), operating ratios produce EBITDA, seller’s discretionary earnings (SDE) follow from EBITDA plus the owner’s pay and add-backs, and a sale multiple on SDE produces the price. Each preset is then cross-checked against sold-transaction data. Where the cross-check and the model disagree, the disagreement is kept in the preset’s note rather than hidden — the general contractor preset is the clearest case (see section 7).

2. The 19 presets and their benchmarks

These are the “typical” lines the calculator itself shows for each industry, word for word:

  • Car wash. Typical mature express site: $1.5-3.0M revenue, 35-50% four-wall EBITDA (MMCG 2026). This default is a solid single site, not a top performer. Price: 4.5x SDE.
  • Auto repair shop. Typical shop: $750K-1.5M revenue, 15-25% net margin. This default is a large 6-tech shop. Price: 2.85x SDE (BizBuySell sold avg).
  • Auto parts store. Typical gross margin 40-45%. Turns here are sales / inventory (COGS turns about 3.2x). Price: 3.0x SDE.
  • Home services. Typical mixed-trade company: 17-21% EBITDA. This default is a good operator at 19%. Price: 2.61x SDE (BizBuySell sold avg).
  • HVAC contractor. Typical HVAC company: 8-14% EBITDA (small shop), 14-20% mixed model. This default is a large 12-tech company at 17%. Price: 2.83x SDE (BizBuySell sold avg).
  • Roofing company. Typical residential replacement: 10-16% EBITDA. This default 13% (midpoint). Price: 2.1x SDE (sold transactions, Legacy ETA 2026).
  • Self-storage. Storage values on cap rate, not an earnings multiple: NOI 65% of revenue at a 6.5% cap rate (sector avg 6.59%). Price: NOI / 6.5%.
  • Dental clinic. Typical dental practice: 15-25% EBITDA; sold median about $558K revenue. This default 20% (midpoint). Price: 2.63x SDE (BizBuySell sold avg).
  • Veterinary clinic. Typical vet clinic: 18-24% EBITDA; private buyers pay 4-6x SDE for a multi-doctor hospital. This default 21% (midpoint). Price: 4.5x SDE.
  • Craft brewery. Typical craft brewery: 21-33% EBITDA at a taproom/distribution blend. This default is a successful small regional at 22% EBITDA. Price: 3.7x SDE.
  • Laundromat. Typical sold store: about $220K revenue, 20-25% EBITDA (BizBuySell median). This default 22% (midpoint). Price: 3.65x SDE.
  • Trucking & Logistics. Typical sold trucking company: about $1.95M revenue, 20.5% SDE margin. This default is a 12-truck carrier at 20.5% EBITDA, the typical sold-carrier margin. Price: 3.0x SDE (BizBuySell sold avg). Cost split per ATRI 2025.
  • 3PL & Warehousing. Typical 3PL: 5-15% EBITDA (up to about 18% tech-enabled). This default 10% (midpoint). Price: 3.0x SDE.
  • Restaurant. Typical sold restaurant: about $718K revenue, 10-20% EBITDA. This default is a solid neighborhood spot. Price: 2.15x SDE (BizBuySell sold avg).
  • Boba & Milk Tea Shop. Typical single boba shop: $250K-450K revenue, 10-25% net margin. This default is a good urban shop at 20% EBITDA. Price: 2.5x SDE.
  • E-commerce Brand. Typical FBA brand: 15-25% net margin; sold median about $1.0M revenue. This default is an established brand at 18% EBITDA. Price: 3.32x SDE (BizBuySell sold avg).
  • General contractor. Typical small GC: $2.1M revenue, 8% EBITDA. Price: 2.56x SDE (BizBuySell 2025, 408 construction sales). Note: sold GCs' SDE runs ~23% of revenue incl. owner comp — add the owner's salary in Seller labor for the owner-operator price.
  • Home health care agency. Typical small agency (non-medical, private-pay): ~$1.1M revenue, 20% EBITDA, sells at ~3.0x SDE (BizBuySell sold median $700K). Price: 3.0x SDE.
  • General business. No benchmark — a blank starting point. Define one consistent unit (job, customer, order, visit, or contract) and use collected revenue and actual expenses.

Some industries also have scale tiers (for example, trucking by fleet size, general contractor by crew count). A tier’s note names its own sources in the model.

3. The research behind the presets

The presets draw on sold-transaction data and trade benchmarks, including: BizBuySell sold listings and medians (2025 and sold averages); the NAHB Member Census 2025; the Siana/CFMA 2026 Benchmarker (1,359 verified statements); ATRI’s Analysis of the Operational Costs of Trucking, 2026 Update (2025 data) and ATBS full-year 2025 owner-operator figures; the ADA Health Policy Institute dental overhead benchmarks; the Coin Laundry Association 2024 Laundry Industry Survey and the 2022 Economic Census (NAICS 812310); the Visiting Angels franchise disclosure document; Genworth/CareScout care rates; the IBBA market data; Legacy ETA 2026 sold transactions; MMCG 2026 car-wash figures; Wise Wash Partners and the Tommy’s Express franchise disclosure document; and 3PL labor benchmarks from Cleverence and MFG Calcs. A source listed here supported the preset or presets named in section 2 — it does not apply to every industry.

4. Shared assumptions

These defaults are the same across presets, and each carries its source on the calculator itself, under “Where the numbers come from”:

  • Loan rate 10% — SBA 7(a) typical: prime 7.00% + 3.0% maximum spread for loans over $350K (Federal Reserve H.15 / SBA, October 2026). Rates move; re-check after the December 8–9, 2026 FOMC meeting.
  • Growth 4% per year — long-run nominal GDP growth, about 3.8–4.1% (Congressional Budget Office, Budget and Economic Outlook 2026–2036, February 2026). Nominal, not real.
  • Tax 25% — US average combined federal + state corporate rate, 25.5% (Tax Foundation, 2026). A C-corporation basis; pass-through owners pay individual marginal rates, so your tax line will differ.
  • Closing costs 2% of price — typical deal costs run 1–4% of price before financing fees (legal + diligence 1–3%). The SBA guaranty fee is modeled separately, and broker fees are seller-paid.
  • Equity 15% — the SBA 7(a) minimum is 10% (5% from your own funds); 15% is a typical buyer contribution.
  • Working capital — receivables, payables, and inventory terms are researched per industry (trucking collects in about 40 days, car wash about 0, general contractor about 75 days plus retainage).

5. The SBA rules applied

The financing math follows SBA SOP 50 10 8.1 (FY2027, effective October 1, 2026) as we understand it:

  • 1.25× DSCR on historical earnings (last fiscal year or two-year average) for acquisitions and buyouts. Projections may no longer carry the test.
  • 10% minimum equity, non-waivable on a first-time acquisition, with at least 5% from the buyer’s own funds.
  • $5M loan cap for SBA 7(a). Deals above it need financing the tools do not model.
  • Guaranty fee on the guaranteed portion of the loan: 85% guaranteed up to $150,000, 75% above. The fee is 2% of the guaranteed portion up to $150,000; 3% up to $700,000; 3.5% up to $1,000,000; and 3.5% on the first $1,000,000 of the guaranteed portion plus 3.75% on the rest above $1,000,000.
  • Fee waiver: the guaranty fee is waived — 0% — for loans up to $700,000 in manufacturing (NAICS 31–33), food supply chain, or rural-area businesses, under SBA Notice 5000-881797 (through September 30, 2027).
  • Quality of Earnings: at a $3M+ purchase price, the lender must engage an independent Quality of Earnings review and will use QoE-adjusted earnings.
  • Seller note: a seller note counts toward the equity requirement only on full standby, capped at 5% of total project cost, with 5% own funds still required.

6. The Deal Score, the two DSCRs, and the SDE worksheet

Two DSCRs. The tools run every deal twice: once on the seller’s SDE, and once on lender-adjusted earnings — after the cost of hiring someone to do the owner’s job, and after an add-back haircut. The lender’s test uses the second one.

Deal Score (0–100). Three financial parts: DSCR, lender-adjusted, scored from 0.75× to 2.25× (45 points); levered IRR, scored from 0% to 45% (35 points); equity multiple, scored from 1× to 4× (20 points). The calculator shows the breakdown under the results, so the score can be checked. Then the risk checks: three questions about owner dependence, customer concentration, and one-off revenue. Each risk you flag takes 5 points off, up to 15, and the breakdown names the risks. The score is a screening device, not a lending decision.

SDE worksheet. Sellers quote SDE from a broker’s recast. The worksheet builds it from the tax return instead — net income plus owner compensation, depreciation and amortization, interest, perks, and one-time items — and shows the gap against the seller’s number as an implied haircut, capped at 50%. A gap over 50% is flagged for diligence rather than applied. The haircut feeds the lender-adjusted DSCR and the Deal Score through the same path as the slider.

What breaks it. The sensitivity line shows the lender-adjusted DSCR if the rate rises one point, and if SDE comes in 10% lower. It is display-only: it changes no input.

7. Caveats, kept in the open

  • Averages are not a business. A preset that fits the industry average can still be wrong for the business in front of you. Price a deal on its own tax returns, not on a preset.
  • Judgment calls exist. Some inputs are researched averages; some are judgment — seasonality shapes, balance-sheet defaults for asset-light businesses, and the split between variable cost and labor. The research notes mark the weakest data as judgment. Where we know a default is unsourced, we say so rather than inventing a source.
  • The general contractor tension. Sold GCs price at about 0.60× revenue, which implies roughly $1.26M on the default revenue — but 2.56× on the default 8% EBITDA gives about $430K. The difference is definitional: sold GCs’ SDE runs about 23% of revenue because owner-operator SDE includes owner compensation and add-backs, while the calculator’s SDE at defaults equals EBITDA. The preset uses the SDE multiple for a consistent method, and the replacement-salary input is the lever that closes the gap for a working owner.
  • Rates and rules move. Prime, the guaranty fee schedule, and SOP requirements change. The shared assumptions carry their dates; check them before relying on a result.
  • Estimates only. Every result depends on the numbers entered and on industry averages. A result that passes today may not pass under a lender’s current requirements. The tools are educational aids, not professional advice and not a loan offer — see the Terms of Use.
